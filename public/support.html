    Casework Pro and subscriptions

    What stays free, what Pro includes, and how to manage billing.

    Always free

    Design unlimited items with every modeling tool. Complete cut lists, nesting diagrams, and item estimate figures stay available on screen.

    Keep two jobs at a time and use them fully: customers, order quantities, the room planner, elevations, and the 3D views. Each job holds two rooms, with up to 40 items in each. The count is the jobs in your library now, so deleting one frees its place. If a subscription ends while you hold more than two jobs, every job stays readable and exportable, and editing returns once you are back within the limit. A job that already has more rooms or items keeps them; only adding more needs Pro.

    Import and export .casework and .caseworkjob files, and create and restore backups, without a subscription. After Pro ends, use Export Job File in a job's menu to keep your whole job.

    What Pro includes

    Pro includes production CSV, PDF, DXF, and cutting-diagram exports; estimate and job-plan PDFs; as many jobs as your shop runs; batch production output; and synchronization through your own iCloud account.

    Billing and renewal

    Choose an annual or monthly subscription. Both offer a 7-day free trial for eligible subscribers. The purchase screen shows your App Store price, billing period, and price after the trial. There is one trial per subscription group.

    Apple charges your Apple Account. Your subscription renews automatically unless you cancel at least 24 hours before the period ends. Cancel a trial at least 24 hours before it ends to avoid being charged. Family Sharing is not included.

    Restore or cancel

    On a new device, use the same Apple Account for App Store purchases. If Pro is missing, open Settings > Casework Pro, or any purchase screen, and choose Restore Purchases. Restoring purchases does not restore your projects; import your files, restore a backup, or enable iCloud sync for those.

    Open Settings > Casework Pro > Manage Subscription to change or cancel your plan with Apple. Canceling stops the next renewal; paid access continues until the current period ends. Your local work and portable files remain yours.

    Working offline

    Once subscribed, Casework checks the on-device App Store receipt and works offline. A failed refresh does not remove your last verified access. Connecting is needed to purchase or restore. Pro access continues during billing grace and billing retry.

    Create your first job

    Start a customer job, set its details, and add reusable items.

    Start from Jobs

    Open the Jobs tab and choose New Job. Give the job a name and, if you use one, a job number. You can add customer, site, schedule, installer, and purchasing details inside the job.

    Casework keeps two jobs without a subscription, each with up to two rooms and 40 items in a room, and the room planner, elevations and order entry all working. Delete a job to free its place. Casework Pro removes the limits; the production files a job produces need Pro either way.

    Casework reopens where you left off. It returns to the Items or Jobs tab you were on, and to the job you had open on its Job Properties, Design or Order Entry tab. Leaving a job with the back arrow forgets it, so the next launch opens the Jobs list. A job you open from the list returns to the tab you last used in it.

    A job contains its own copies of every cabinet or assembly you add. Editing an item in a job does not change the reusable item in your Item Library.

    In Job Properties, Door and drawer fronts follows Cabinet Standards and contains the front styles, materials, thicknesses, and small drawer-front cutoff. Scheduling holds the installer and dates. Parameters is the last page.

    Add items

    Add a starting item from the shipped catalogue or your Item Library, set its quantity, then open it to edit the construction. Use the Items tab when you want to create or maintain a reusable starting point for future jobs.

    The shipped frameless collection is shelved by family: Base and its corners, Upper with its corners and floating shelves, Tall, Vanity, and Panel & Filler. A vanity is the base collection's construction at bathroom height, so it follows the vanity height and depth in your standard sizes rather than a kitchen base's. Item descriptions say how a cabinet is built, not how big it is — open an item to read its size in your own units, because every one of them resizes when a job applies its standards.

    In Items, tap a shipped item to inspect its construction in the read-only 3D viewer. Its actions menu also offers View details and Copy to My Items for an editable copy.

    In Items, and when you add items to a job, the row under Search shows one library at a time: All, My Items, Essentials, Frameless Toe Kick, Frameless Legs or Range hoods. Each says how many items it holds for the current search and tags, so a search that finds nothing in the chosen library still shows where it did find something. Casework remembers the library you chose. An item's picture includes the oven, microwave or other appliance it is built around.

    Save to Item Library, beside the save indicator in an open item's top bar, copies what is on screen into your Item Library under a name you choose. It is always a copy: the item you were editing stays where it was, and the two are separate from that moment. This is how a cabinet you built inside a customer's job becomes a reusable starting point.

    Frameless — Adjustable Legs is the frameless floor cabinets on adjustable plastic legs instead of an integrated toe kick: every Base, Base Corner, Tall and Vanity item has a legged twin with the same name and size and an item code ending -LG. The sides stop at the 4½ in leg height, with no notch and no toe board, and each cabinet carries its levellers in the model. By default legs stand 50 mm in from each side and from the front and back, each set on its own: four at the corners, a centred pair at the front and back for each 36 in of width beyond the first, six under a 90° or 45° corner, and you can change these numbers at four levels (see Legs and plinth settings below). Change a cabinet's width and the centre legs are added or removed to match. Plinth boards are added in the room, not in the item.

    Legs and plinth settings set where adjustable legs stand, the width at which a cabinet gets a centred pair, what one leg costs, and how a new plinth starts. Leg inset is set from the sides and from the front and back separately, and a note appears under the fields when a combination cannot work, such as a plinth board that would touch the legs. The shop's are in Settings, under New job defaults, and each new job copies them. The job's are in Job Properties, Cabinet Standards, Legs and Plinth. A room's are in its Plan panel under Cabinet properties, Standards, Legs and Plinth, and an item's under Legs in its settings, for an item on legs: turn off Use job values, or Use room / job values, to give that room or item its own. The nearest level that has its own numbers applies: item, then room, then job. Changing a number lays out again every set of legs nobody has edited; a set you added to or took from is left as it is. A room's numbers apply to an item that stands in that room only. A plinth keeps the values it was made with, and Use default values in the plinth restores the room's.

    Toe kick height and Toe kick recess are set beside them, at the same four levels: the numbers a new toe kick, or a cabinet switched onto legs in Design, starts from. Legs are as tall as the toe kick height, so one number answers both. An item already built keeps its height until you edit it.

    Each leg is a hardware line in the estimate and the bill of materials, Cabinet leg leveller, counted for every cabinet and its quantity and priced at the leg price in those same settings. With no leg price the line is shown unpriced.

    Range hoods are their own collection, apart from the frameless cabinets. A straight hood resizes by its overall width, height and depth, and its ledge, duct and stretcher widths are named parameters you can change. The curved hoods are drawn at the published series sizes, 3636 and 4236. None of them follows the standard sizes a job applies.

    A picture also shows the handles or knobs fitted to the item. Items without pulls stay without them.

    Choose what to make

    Order Entry lists every item in the job, and the ones with a tick are what Estimate, Bill of Materials, Drawings, Cut Diagrams and Export make. The bar above the list narrows it: Name or code searches item names and codes, a chip for each room shows the items placed there (All rooms shows every item, and Not placed appears while some item is in no room), and the All types menu keeps Base, Upper, Tall, Vanity or Other. Each chip and type counts what it would show.

    The filter is the selection: choosing one ticks exactly the items it shows and nothing else. You can still tick or untick single items after that, and Select All and Clear act on what is shown. Choose a room and the outputs make only what is placed in that room, not the whole quantity: the bar says Outputs build only what is placed in the room, and each item says how many it builds for it. Spares placed in no room are left out. Show All goes back to every item. Order Entry opens on every item each time.

    Design an item

    Change dimensions, add parts, and keep relationships solvable.

    Select what you mean

    An item opens in Design when it is a cabinet, and in Advanced otherwise. The switch in the middle of the top bar goes between three ways of working: Design lays the cabinet out on one front drawing, as Design a cabinet describes; Advanced shows the model in 3D with every part unlocked, for the parts, relationships and machining the layout does not make; and Inspect shows the same model to look over without changing anything.

    In Advanced, tap a part in the model or choose it in the Parts list; a part tapped in the model is found in the list, which scrolls to it and opens its group. Its inspector has three pages: Size, for its cut size, position and relationships; Material, for its sheet, finish, edge banding and front style; and Machining, for its operations, shape, joints and hardware. The bin beside its name deletes it. Dimensions may accept arithmetic and units. Casework stores physical sizes in millimetres and converts what you see when a project uses inches.

    Inspect an item

    Inspect, the third choice at the top, shows the item in 3D to check before it is cut, and changes nothing in it. Tap a part in the model or in the Parts list and the panel on the right says what it is: its cut size, material, grain, edge banding, joinery, machining and notes. With nothing picked, the panel shows the item's width, height and depth and how many parts it has.

    The eye beside each part in the list hides it, and the eye on a group hides the whole group. Show only this hides everything but the picked part, and Show all brings everything back. The tape measure in the top bar measures between corners and edges, as in Advanced, and Fit view frames the parts still showing. Edit in Advanced takes the picked part to Advanced, still picked. The same view opens when you inspect an item in a job's list, or a shipped item in Items.

    Add parts

    Parts are added from the Parts list on the left. Add a panel arms the model: tap a face and a panel is placed against it, and tapping empty space puts the tool away. Add a drawer builds a drawer bank inside the Box. Add 3D object opens the catalog to insert an appliance or other bought-in model inside the Box. Add a door hangs a door on the carcass, hinges and all. Each new part appears in the list, where you can rename it, size it and move it like any other.

    Add operations to a part

    Operations are the machining done to a part: cuts, holes and edge work. Choose a part in the Parts list, or tap it in the model, open Machining in the inspector and choose Add an operation. The list is grouped into Cuts, such as a dado or a rabbet, Holes, such as a single hole or a hole pattern, and Edges. Choosing one places a default straight away and opens its editor.

    In the editor, set the operation's size, position and depth. Each operation stays in the list under Operations with a short summary: tap the row to edit it again, the eye to suppress it without deleting it, or the bin to delete it. An operation that a hinge or another relationship made is shown, but its numbers cannot be changed; several alike, such as the holes a runner bores, are one row, Hole × 12, that opens to list them.

    Relationships

    Constraints hold parts to faces, openings, offsets, and even spacing. If a requested move would break those relationships, Casework refuses the invalid result and explains the conflict instead of silently changing the design.

    Operations that repeat

    A hole pattern's columns and rows, and a line-boring row's hole count, each run from 1 to 200. An operation that asks for more is capped, and one set below 1 bores nothing at all; both are reported against the operation's own row with the number that was asked for. You will only see these on a file written elsewhere, because the editor's own control cannot leave the range.

    Project settings

    The gear inside the editor changes this item or job: units, cutting gap, materials, edge banding, and hardware. The Settings tab changes the defaults copied into new work.

    Design a cabinet

    Shape a cabinet, lay out its doors and drawers, and its shelves and partitions, on one screen.

    Open it

    A cabinet opens in Design, the first of the three choices at the top: Design to lay the cabinet out, Advanced to change any part by hand, and Inspect to look it over. Face, Interior, Cabinet and Shape, under the top bar, choose what the drawing shows, and the tools for what you pick stand beside it. Nothing changes in the item until you tap Build: Not built yet says there is something to build, Discard throws it away, and Build is one step to undo.

    Undo and Redo in the top bar take back the last change to the drawing, one at a time, and then the last build. Going to Advanced or Inspect, opening an output such as the cut list, or going back to the job builds what is drawn first; if it cannot be built, the editor stays on what needs fixing and says so. With nothing picked, Face shows the cabinet's width, height and depth, which can be typed while nothing is waiting to be built.

    Face and Interior draw the cabinet's overall width across the top of the drawing and its height up the left, toe kick included, in the units the top bar shows.

    Face and Interior are offered on the cabinet's own Box and on a Box you add inside it, while Box drives is chosen. A Box that belongs to a drawer, a door or a bought-in object has Shape only.

    A Box inside a cabinet starts from what already stands in it, read the same way as the cabinet's own Box, and Build makes only what its own layout makes. If its size is worked out from the parts around it, Casework asks you to give it a size of its own first, because its lines could not follow a change.

    Shape

    Shape is the profile editor: draw the cabinet from the Front, the Side or the Top. Tap a run to choose what it is — Front, Back, a side, Top or Bottom — or mark it Open when nothing is built on it, such as the open front of a shelf unit. An open run is drawn dashed and is not offered as a surface for a panel.

    On a shaped cabinet, Face and Interior draw it as it is cut. A door or drawer front follows the outline and stands the same reveal off a sloped or curved run as off its square edges, inset or overlaid, and a curve stays a curve in the cut list and the DXF. Its hinges are spaced up the length of edge the shape leaves it.

    Beside a sloped or curved run, rows are shared by the height halfway across the bay and columns by the width halfway up it, so equal rows hold about as much as each other; an inner drawer, roll-out tray or closet rod there stops where the shape does. A shaped cabinet whose shelves and partitions could not be laid out before opens with them ready to edit.

    Cabinets under a sloped ceiling or stair

    A cabinet can follow a sloped ceiling, a knee wall or the underside of a stair. Open it in Design and choose Shape, then draw it from the view where the slope shows: the Side for a ceiling or stair that rises toward the back, the Front for one that rises sideways.

    Tap the run to slope, usually the Top, and choose Break to split it where the slope begins. Drag the new corner to where the slope meets the side, or select the sloped run and type its Length and Angle; Angle is measured from the horizontal.

    Build makes the cabinet as drawn. In Face and Interior, doors and drawer fronts follow the slope and stand the same reveal off it, and shelves, partitions and inner drawers stop where the shape does. A room can have a sloped ceiling too (see Sloped ceilings under Plan a room), and Fit to ceiling there shapes a cabinet to it for you.

    A framed cabinet follows the slope with its frame, when it is drawn from the Front or the Side with straight runs across the top no steeper than 60°. Drawn from the Front, the top rail follows the slope at its full width measured square to the slope, the stiles are cut to it at the top, and a top that is flat and then slopes gets two rail pieces glued in a mitre at the break. Doors and drawer fronts under it are cut to follow the rail with the fit's overlay or gap, and a mid rail or mullion that would sit wholly above it is left out. Drawn from the Side, the frame comes down only where the slope crosses its top at the front. An arch or a step, a drawing from the Top, or one from both the Front and the Side leaves the cabinet frameless.

    Face: openings

    Face divides the front into openings. Tap one, then a number under Split: beside Rows it stacks that many openings one above the other, and beside Columns it puts them side by side, from two to six. Each new opening is what this one was, so a bank of three drawers is Drawer, then 3 beside Rows. A new bank of drawers leads with a short top drawer, 5⅞ in, and the others share the rest equally; Equal makes them all one height. Splitting an opening that is already in a stack adds to the stack rather than nesting a new one. When something else holds Build back, the tools say so, and Show the opening picks it.

    Choose what closes the opening: Open, Door, Pair of doors, Drawer, False front, Blank panel or Appliance. A door is hinged left or right. A drawer is built with its box, runner and front, to the shop's drawer settings; a false front is a fixed panel with no hardware.

    Each opening's size is its front's size. Held keeps that size when the cabinet is resized; Shares divides what the held sizes leave equally with the other sharing openings. Type a size to hold it, tap Equal to share all of them again, and Whole column or Whole row to pick the split the opening is in. Drag the line between two openings to move it, or tap it and type how far it is from the edge.

    A drawer in a stack takes its width from its column, so under its own height there is Column width: Held there keeps the whole column at that width. Hold both drawer stacks of a vanity and the doors between them take up the resize. A front in a row split into columns has Row height the same way. A lock in the corner marks every front a held size keeps.

    With nothing picked, the Reveals are the gaps at the top, the bottom, the ends, and between two fronts. A cabinet that was not laid out here starts with the gaps its fronts already have.

    Each opening is labelled in its corner: a number for its column, from the left, and a letter for its place in that column, from the top — 1A, 1B, 2A. The labels follow the layout, so a split or a join renumbers them. They are shown in the editor only.

    Face: detail of one front

    Tap a door, a pair or a drawer and open Front detail. A door has its Fit — full overlay, half overlay or inset — and its number of Hinges, automatic until you pick one. A drawer has its Pieces, Corners and Bottom, its Runner length (the longest that fits the cabinet until you pick a shorter one, kept while it fits) and its Clearances: bottom, top, side and front, from the slide preset until you type one.

    A door or a drawer can have its handle placed: Set handle position, then its distance from the free edge (the left edge on a drawer) and from the top, measured to the handle's centre, and whether it lies Horizontal or Vertical. Use the job's puts it back on the job's rule when the cabinet is built.

    Each of these follows the job, or the slide, until you change it, and each one you change is listed under Overrides with Use settings to put it back. A handle set from the edge of a drawer cannot be mirrored, so Mirror leaves such a cabinet as it is.

    Face: reveals on one front

    Tap an opening that has a front, then Own reveals to give that front its own gaps at the top, the bottom, the left and the right. Only that front's edges move, inside its own opening: its neighbours stay where they are. Use the face's reveals puts it back on the face's gaps.

    A front keeps its own reveals only while it is one opening. Split it and its pieces follow the face's reveals again.

    Frame members

    On a framed cabinet, Face draws the frame under the fronts, each stile, rail and mullion as a band, with the fronts lighter so the frame shows through. Every line between two fronts is a member: a mid rail across, a mullion up and down. Tap the line and choose No member when two fronts should meet with nothing between them; a pair of doors has the same choice.

    Tap a stile, the top or bottom rail, or a pair's mullion to pick it, and type its Width under Frame member; a mid rail or mullion on a line has the same field under Member. The style's width is shown beside it, and Reset to style puts it back. A width you type stays through a resize and a change of fit. If the planner widened a stile for a scribe or a gap it says so, and typing a width replaces the widening.

    A stile and the top and bottom rails also have Overhang: how far the member runs past the case — a stile past the case side, the top rail up above where the style places it, the bottom rail down below it. Overhang moves the member's outer edge out and leaves its inner edge, the opening and the fronts where they are; Width grows a member into the opening. A stile starts at the style's outside overhang and a rail at the style's rail overhang for the cabinet's kind; Reset to style under Overhang puts it back. A finished end flush with the frame's face stops behind a stile that overhangs it. A scribe the planner adds to a stile against a wall stands past its overhang; a gap it closed between cabinets is cleared by setting an overhang, and the planner offers it again. Under a sloped top the top rail follows the slope and takes no overhang. The drawing shows a width or an overhang as soon as you change it; Build makes it.

    With nothing picked, Front fit sets this cabinet's fit: Style, which follows the job's style, or Overlay, Full overlay, Inset or Beaded inset. The line under it gives the style's overlay or gap for that fit, which takes the place of the Reveals on a framed cabinet. A cabinet changed to inset or beaded inset takes the style's inset widths, except for members whose width you typed.

    Changing the frame keeps the fronts' sizes. When a cabinet is framed, or its fit, a member's width or a No member changes, the fronts keep their height and width and one takes up the difference: the bottom opening of a stack, and among fronts side by side a door, or else the widest. Fronts that share the space shrink together. A drawer under a built-in microwave or oven cannot keep its height without running into the deck the appliance sits on, so there the drawer and the doors in its stack give up the room for the rails instead, and the deck and fixed shelves stay where they are. Where a fixed shelf, stretcher or partition stands behind a line, as in a vanity with a sink bay or the Coffee Cabinet, the member stays on it and the fronts beside it give up its width. If a front would end up too small to build, nothing changes and Casework says so.

    Front height and Front width give a framed cabinet's front as it will be built, and a size typed there builds that front. The labels on the drawing do the same; a door cut to a slope gives its width and its high and low height.

    Interior: shelves, partitions and drawers

    Interior divides the inside into bays. Tap a bay, then a number beside Rows to put that many shelves across it, or beside Columns to put partitions up and down it; the menu under each says what every new line is. Tap a line to choose what stands on it: across, Adjustable shelf, Fixed shelf, Stretcher, Top, Bottom or Separator; up and down, Partition, Double partition or Separator. A separator builds nothing and only divides the space. A double partition is two boards back to back, so a drawer on each side has a panel for its runner.

    Nosed adjustable shelf and Nosed fixed shelf put a solid-wood nosing on the shelf's front edge instead of edge banding. The shelf is cut shallower by the nosing's depth, and the nosing is a part of its own, cut from the solid wood chosen as the job's Shelf nosing material, hard maple unless you pick another. A job made before that setting follows its door material instead: walnut doors give a walnut nosing. Its depth, its height and whether it sits flush with the top, centred or flush with the bottom are set in the assembly style, under Shelves & rails; until a style says, it is 1 in by 1 in, flush with the top. A style can nose every shelf, and then a line chosen as a plain shelf stays plain.

    Sizes inside are clear sizes, between the members. Line up with face holds a member behind the reveal in front of it, so a fixed shelf stays under the drawer it carries when that drawer is resized.

    A bay can hold an Inner drawer: a drawer with no front, set back to clear the door. Choosing it in an open space places one at the bottom of the space, as tall as the Inner drawer height, and leaves the rest open above it; a number beside Inner drawers, under Split, places two to six. Nothing is built between them: the lines are separators. Pick a drawer and its Height changes how much space it takes, and Standard puts it back. In a bay too short to leave any open, the drawers share it.

    Inner drawer height is set in Settings for new jobs, in a job's properties, under Fronts in a room's Cabinet properties, which beats the job's, and in an item's own settings. It is used when a drawer is placed: a drawer already placed keeps its height until you change it.

    How far an inner drawer is set back from the carcass front is Inner drawer setback: in Settings for new jobs, in a job's properties, and under Fronts in a room's Cabinet properties, which beats the job's. Tap an inner drawer and type its Set back to give that one drawer its own; Use settings goes back to the job's. The setback is used when the drawer is built, so drawers already built keep theirs until they are built again.

    The fronts are drawn over the interior as dashed outlines. A drawer cannot have a shelf, stretcher or partition running through it. When an adjustable shelf meets a drawer, Make it a fixed shelf turns it into one and lines it up with the drawer's reveal; when a fixed member runs through one, Line it up with the reveal moves it there.

    Cabinet: the case

    Cabinet, the tab after Face and Interior, shows how this cabinet is built on cards side by side. Case has its Top (a full top, or stretchers for a worktop to go over), whether it has a Bottom, its Back (Grooved, Rabbeted, Applied to the rear edges, No back, or Butted, which is how the older starters such as Base Cabinet build it: fitted between the sides and flush with the rear), the Nailers behind the back, the Back inset, and the Back stretcher. A cabinet on a toe kick or legs keeps its bottom, and one built some other way is left as it is.

    Each row says beside its name where its value comes from: Following the settings for this job, Set for this item, or As this item was built. A row you change has Use settings to put it back. The top follows the settings for its class of cabinet until you choose one: Top: base cabinets, upper cabinets and tall cabinets in Settings for new jobs, in a job's properties and in a room's settings, which beats the job's. Nothing changes until Build, and a cabinet you have not chosen anything for keeps the top it was built with.

    With nailers the back stands off the rear by their sheet. Without them it stands in by the Inset from the rear, 12 mm until you type another. An applied back has no nailers and no inset: the sides, the bottom and the top stop at its inside face. With no back the shelves stop at the nailers, or at the rear of the case.

    Left end and Right end finish what shows at each side: Unfinished, an Applied panel, or a Finished sheet side. An applied panel is cut from the Finished ends sheet, set in Materials, and stands outside the cabinet, flush with the door face and down to the floor, so the carcass and the run keep their width. A finished sheet side is the side itself cut from that sheet. On a toe kick, the Left and Right toe kick end say what closes the recess: Unfinished leaves the notch open, Finished adds a small board of the finished sheet, Notched end is an applied panel with the toe kick cut in it, and Full end is an applied panel run whole to the floor.

    Fronts shows the face's four reveals, the same numbers as the Face tab, following the job's reveal setting until changed; Use settings puts them back. Materials lists what each kind of part is cut from, and Hardware each kind of hardware, as pickers over the job's sheets and presets. They change at once, not when the cabinet is built: Discard does not take them back, and Undo does. A cabinet in a job follows the job's materials and its hardware as a whole, so choosing one sheet makes that standard the cabinet's own, with the rest as the job gave it; it is listed under Overrides, and Follow the job gives it back.

    Every choice here is listed under Overrides with a Revert, and Mirror carries them across with the cabinet, swapping left and right.

    Assembly styles

    A style is the one place your shop's construction is answered. Edit a style a page at a time: Carcass (the top for each kind of cabinet, the bottom, how the top and bottom meet the sides: between them, running over them or mitred), Joints, Back & nailers (the back, nailers and their height, and where the back stands: touching the wall, behind nailers or a set distance in), Toe kick & legs (notched sides, a ladder base of rails and rungs, or adjustable legs, with the height and recess), Shelves & rails (stretcher width, a flat drawer rail under the top drawer or between every drawer, shelf setbacks and clearance), Drawers (the top drawer's front, inner drawers, the drawer box), Finished ends (an applied panel, a finished side, or a panel in the door style), Fronts (the reveals) and Banding (which edges of each kind of part, and with what tape). Any row can be left As built.

    Frameless — solid wood, one of the shipped styles, starts a solid-wood shop off: every shelf nosed 1 in by 1 in, flush with the top, and finished ends made in the door style.

    A job keeps its own copy of its style. Every cabinet added to the job, from the library or started in it, is built with that copy, so the library is built your way. To build one customer's job differently, say a 4 in toe kick, open Job Properties, Cabinet Standards, and tap Edit this job's style: the shop's style is not changed, and Save as a shop style keeps the copy for later. After a change the page says how many cabinets were built before it, and Rebuild all builds them again, as one step you can undo. Cabinets that chose their own style are left alone.

    To start an item of your own, choose An empty carcass, built your way on the New Item screen: pick a style, the kind of cabinet and its width, height and depth, and Create. The carcass is built as the style says and opens in Design, ready for its doors, drawers and shelves.

    Assembly styles describe how your shop puts a cabinet together. Open Settings, then Assembly styles, and tap New style: a few questions, each with pictures, ask how the sides meet the bottom, and how they meet a full top and the stretchers (groove, dowels, screws through or pocket screws, or don't model the joint). The last screen shows what it builds on a 30 in base cabinet; name it and Save. Three styles ship with the app and can be copied.

    A style can also say how the case is built (the top for base, upper and tall cabinets, the back and whether it has nailers), how rows of dowels, screws and pocket screws are laid out (a set number, or as many as a spacing needs, with the distance in from each end, in millimetres or inches), and how a drawer box is made (4 or 5 piece, which parts run through, grooved or applied bottom). Applying a style to a cabinet changes the case and rebuilds its drawers to match, then makes the joints; new drawers start the style's way. Anything the style leaves out stays as built.

    In Settings, Assembly styles, a style's menu has Use for new jobs. A new job takes a copy of the style you choose on the New Job screen (the shop's default is ticked); under Job Properties, Cabinet Standards, you can change the job's style, update it when the shop's has changed, and Apply to all cabinets in this job, which can be undone until you change the job again. A cabinet in a job says in its Cabinet section whether it was built with the job's style, and Use the job's style puts it back; a cabinet built with another style is listed under Overrides.

    In Design, Cabinet has an Assembly style row. Choose a style and its joints are made on that cabinet at once, replacing the ones a style made before; joints you made by hand are kept. Each joint is machined in both parts, appears on the drawings and the DXF, and is added to the hardware list and the estimate. A cabinet that no style has touched is built as before, and a style never changes the fronts, the hardware you picked or parts you drew by hand.

    Face frames

    A style can build its cabinets with a face frame. On the style's Carcass page choose Face frame instead of Frameless, and a Face frame page follows it. The shipped Face frame style starts from a ¾ in frame with 1½ in stiles and rails, flush with the carcass sides outside, and fronts that overlay it by ½ in. Every shipped cabinet builds either way, so a framed job uses the same items: choose the style for the job in Job Properties, Cabinet Standards, and Apply to all cabinets in this job.

    Members sets the Frame thickness, the Outside overhang, which is how far an outer stile stands past the carcass side (zero is flush outside, and a negative overhang makes the frame narrower than the carcass), and the width of the stiles, top rail, bottom rail, mid rails and mullions. There are two sets of widths, one for overlay and full overlay and one for inset and beaded inset; a cabinet takes the set for its fit.

    Rails by cabinet places the top and bottom rails of base, upper and tall cabinets separately. The top rail is flush with the carcass top or with the Top's underside; the bottom rail is flush with the carcass bottom or with the Bottom's top face, where it laps down over the Bottom's front edge. An offset moves a rail from there toward the inside of the frame, and a negative one moves it out past the carcass.

    Each rail also has an overhang: it moves only the rail's outer edge out from there — the top rail's up, the bottom rail's down — and the rail is cut that much wider, so the openings and the fronts stay where they are. It starts at 0, and a cabinet can set its own under Frame member in Design. Whatever of the frame stands above the case top counts against the room's ceiling, and a sloped one, in the planner.

    Fronts sets the fit new cabinets start with. Overlay fronts sit on the face of the frame and lap each member by the Overlay, ½ in to start. Full overlay fronts cover the frame up to the Gap between full-overlay fronts, ⅛ in. Inset fronts sit in their opening, flush with the face of the frame, with the Gap around an inset front all round. Beaded inset is inset with a bead, ¼ in, run on the inside edges of the members, and the front is fitted inside the bead. A pair of doors meets on a mullion unless the style says otherwise, and any pair can say otherwise in Design.

    Joinery says how the frame goes together and onto the carcass. Stiles and rails are joined with pocket screws from the back, dowels or dominos: two to a joint, or one where the member that runs in is 1½ in wide or less, and both numbers are yours. Frame to the carcass is Glued and nailed, Pocket screws or Biscuits. Brads and biscuits are one hardware line per cabinet, one for each spacing started along every stile and rail glued on: every 6 in for nails, every 8 in for biscuits. Pocket screws are driven from inside the carcass panel behind the frame.

    Hardware deals with a stile that stands proud of the carcass side. Drawer slides go on Spacer blocks, strips of the frame's wood as thick as the stile stands proud, either a Full-length strip or Front and rear blocks, or on Rear brackets, a set per drawer in the hardware list. Each fit has its hinge: the face-frame concealed hinge with ½ in overlay for overlay and full overlay, and the inset face-frame hinge for inset and beaded inset. A 2½ in butt hinge ships as well.

    Runs and ends holds the Scribe allowance, ½ in, added to a stile against a wall on the wall side only; what closes the space Between two framed cabinets: Widen both stiles, Widen one stile or A filler (see Face frames in a run under Plan a room); and whether a Finished end stands flush with the frame's face or behind the frame. How the end itself is made is on the Finished ends page.

    Corner cabinets are framed too. On a 90° corner the two corner stiles meet in a glued 45° mitre. On a 45° corner each return has a stile of its own, the diagonal frame's stiles are bevelled 22½° to meet them, and the diagonal of the carcass is set back so each return shows a full stile. A blind corner's blank panel is framed by the end stile and a mullion, and the door takes up the rest. A lazy Susan corner has no corner stiles, so its trays can turn: the top and bottom rails run into the inside corner and are mitred there.

    A cabinet the frame cannot be built on, such as one drawn with an arch or a step, or a lazy Susan corner whose stiles leave no room for the smallest kit, stays frameless. The message after Apply to all or Rebuild all names each cabinet left frameless, so you know which ones need no frame stock.

    A framed cabinet's drawings end with a Face frame sheet: the frame seen from the front, chained dimensions through every member and the overall size, each clear opening, the pocket holes dashed, and a table of the members to cut with their sizes and wood. The bill of materials, on screen and in the PDF, and the cut-list CSV add Face frame stock: one line for each wood and width to rip, with the length and board feet to buy, waste included, and the number of pieces. Stiles, rails, mullions and spacer blocks are counted there; fillers keep their own line.

    The frame's wood is the Face frame row under Materials, hard maple 4/4 to start, set for the shop, the job or one cabinet like any other material. The estimate adds a Face frames line: Face frame labour, ¾ h per framed cabinet to start, at the Face frame rate, both in Settings, Estimating.

    Joining cabinets, at the end of the Face frame page, says how framed cabinets standing side by side share a frame at their seam: One frame per run, Shared stile or Split stile. Joined face frames, under Plan a room, explains it.

    Appliances, glass and the rest

    Choose Blank panel to close an opening with a plain panel. It is made from the doors' sheet, so it matches them, and banded on every edge.

    Choose Appliance to hold a built-in microwave, oven or other integrated appliance in an opening. Pick the appliance, then set the gap on each side, the gap behind it and how far it stands back from the front. The appliance is the opening less those gaps, so it follows the opening when the cabinet is resized. A negative gap laps its trim over the opening, and a negative set back stands it proud of the front.

    A glass door is a door style. Tap a door, a pair, a drawer or a false front and open its door style: choose a glass style such as Slab glass or Shaker glass, and the glass is listed under Doors and glass on the bill of materials.

    In a bay, choose Roll-out tray for a low drawer box on slides at the bottom of the bay, set back to clear the door's hinges like an inner drawer. Tray height is the drawer box from bottom to top, 4 in until you type another. Choose Closet rod for a rod across the bay: Rod drop is the distance from the top of the bay to the middle of the rod. The rod is bought and listed under Hardware, never cut.

    Cubbies divides a bay into a grid in one step: tap the square in the grid for the rows and columns you want, and fixed shelves go across the bay with partitions between them in each row. Every cubby is still a bay of its own, so it can be resized, held or given a tray.

    Tap a line inside and choose Glass shelf for a shelf bought to size: it rests on the same pins as a wood shelf and is listed with its size, never cut. A shelf also has Set back, Side clearance and Holes only, no shelf, which bores the panels for a shelf and makes none. A partition or a fixed shelf has Set back. Use default puts one back.

    Delete a front, an appliance, a rod or an inner drawer in Advanced and its opening goes back to Open, or its bay to empty, so the next build does not make it again.

    Glass shelves and closet rods are priced in Settings, under Estimating, in Bought-in parts: glass by its area and a rod by its length. They start at benchmark rates; change them, or clear one and that part is on the estimate with its quantity and counted as a missing price.

    On the drawing

    Touch and hold an opening, a bay or a line on the drawing, or right-click it on a Mac or with a trackpad, for a menu of what can be done to it. On an opening or a bay: Horizontal split and Vertical split, with the same choices as the buttons; what closes it or what is in it; Hold its size or Let it share the space; Make them equal; Join the column or Join the row; and Delete the opening or Delete the bay, which gives its space to the one beside it. On a line: what stands on it, Make them equal and Remove line. VoiceOver offers the same as actions on each opening, bay and line.

    With a keyboard, on an iPad or a Mac: H splits the picked opening or bay into two rows and V into two columns, and a number from 2 to 6 straight after makes it that many; a number on its own splits into that many rows. E makes them equal, L holds its size or lets it share, and Delete deletes it, or removes a picked line. The arrow keys pick the opening or bay beside, and Shift with an arrow moves a picked line by one step: a sixteenth of an inch, or a millimetre.

    Pick several, under the split buttons, turns taps into picking: tap more openings or bays to add them, or a picked one to take it out. With a keyboard, Shift with an arrow adds the one beside. With several picked, choose what closes them or what is in them, a door's hinge, Hold their sizes or Let them share, and they all change at once; E, L and Delete act on all of them. Done picking goes back to one.

    A line you drag catches on the lines level with it, on the reveal in front of it when you drag a shelf inside, and, under a drawer, where that drawer is a 5⅞ in short top. A dashed line across the Box shows what it has caught on.

    Toe kick and legs

    The toe kick is drawn hatched under the face and labelled Toe kick or Legs. Tap it to edit what the cabinet stands on. On a cabinet that can be changed, choose Toe kick or Legs. A toe kick has a Height and a Recess: the deck, the notch in each side and the toe board move together, and the fronts start at its top. Legs have a Height: the deck and the sides stand that high on adjustable levellers, and each leg stretches to suit.

    Both start from the settings. Use settings returns the item to the numbers set for its job, room or shop, and the line under the fields says whether it follows them. Build keeps the item's own number only where it differs from the job's, so an item that follows the settings goes on following them.

    For a cabinet on legs, Leg placement gives this item its own leg inset from the sides, its own inset from the front and back, and its own span before it gets a centred pair; the legs are laid out again as you change them. Changing a number in the settings changes what Use settings returns and what a new switch starts from. An item already built keeps its height until you edit it here.

    Corner cabinets and cabinets built some other way cannot be switched between a toe kick and legs, and a toe kick built by hand is left as it is. Its parts can still be changed in the viewport.

    Overrides

    On the Cabinet tab, and in Advanced with nothing selected, Overrides lists every way this cabinet differs from its settings and from the library item it came from, each in a sentence with the standard beside it: a toe kick or leg number the job does not use, an inner drawer's own setback, a front with its own reveals, its own door style, a job standard it does not follow, a different size, a face or interior laid out differently, carcass parts added or taken out. They wait while something is not built yet.

    Use settings puts one difference back on the settings, and Use settings for all puts them all back. Revert puts a size back to the library item's; Use the library's face or interior lays it out as the library item is. Reset to library item, after asking, puts the whole cabinet back to the library item at its present size. Each is one step to undo.

    A front given its own door, drawer front or small drawer front style in the inspector is listed too, named by its opening, such as Front 1B, with the cabinet's style beside it. Use the cabinet's puts it back. A front's own style is kept when the face is built again, unless the opening becomes another kind of front.

    Mirror

    Mirror left for right, under Overrides, turns the whole cabinet the other way round: a corner fits the other corner, the doors are hung from the other side, finished ends and the face and interior change sides, and names say Left for Right. It is one step to undo.

    Every mirror is measured before it is kept: each part and each hole must stand exactly where the original's would in a mirror, or nothing changes and the inspector says why. A Box with a drawn shape and a part at an angle with holes in it, such as a 45° corner's door, cannot be mirrored yet.

    A mirrored cabinet remembers that it is mirrored. Overrides compares it with its library item mirrored the same way, so the mirror itself is not listed, and Use the library's face and Reset to library item keep it on its new side.

    What is built

    Build makes the parts and every relationship that holds them: an opening Box behind each front, held to the sides, the deck, the rails and the members beside it, and the drawers, doors and shelves built on those. Resize the cabinet and the held fronts keep their size while the shared ones divide the rest. Pins, hinges and runners are placed as they are for parts added by hand; a runner beside a partition is bored into the partition.

    A cabinet that was not made in the editor opens with its fronts, shelves and partitions read as openings and bays. Change the face and only the fronts are rebuilt; change the interior and both are. If a face or interior cannot be read that way, the editor says so and offers to clear it.

    A Box inside a cabinet has its own Face and Interior. Select the Box in Advanced, then choose Edit the face & interior in its inspector. Its openings and shelves are measured inside that Box and move with it; it has no toe kick.

    Clear the face or Clear the interior, under the tools with nothing picked, empties it after you confirm, to lay it out again. Parts you added by hand are not the layout's and are never removed by it.

    A relationship the layout made that you change by hand in Advanced, such as a drawer's Box moved or its position typed, is kept when the cabinet is resized. When a build would make it again, it asks first: Keep the hand edits puts each back on the edge or board built in its place, the same distance off, and drops one whose line is gone; Build from the layout replaces them. A door or drawer front deleted in Advanced makes its opening Open, so building does not put it back; Undo brings both back.

    A part that goes through a side of its drawer's or opening's Box, such as a drawer box longer than the bay it slides into, is listed under Issues. Show selects the part.

    Plan a room

    Lay out rooms, walls, openings, appliances, and cabinet runs.

    Lay out the room

    Open a job and choose Design. In Plan, open Items & room. Add to room comes first: Add item places a cabinet from the order or the Item Library, and the tiles below it add a 3D object, countertop, wall, door or window, molding, plinth, or field condition. Room settings follow, folded into four rows that each say what they are set to: Room holds the name, height, snap grid and wall thickness; Surfaces the floor, wall, countertop and backsplash finishes; Cabinet properties what the room changes about the job's cabinets; and Estimate whether its countertops are estimated. Cabinet properties opens a sheet with the same pages as Job Properties — Standards, Fronts, Materials, Finish & Banding, Hardware, Joints and Assembly style — where each value follows the job until you change it for the room: a dot marks a room value, and Reset to job follows the job again. A cabinet's own settings still beat the room's. Room width and depth are measured from the walls; move a wall to change them.

    A new room starts at round numbers in the unit the job is set to: 4800 by 3600 mm with a 2400 ceiling, a 25 mm grid and 140 mm walls in millimetres, or 16 by 12 feet with an 8 foot ceiling, a half-inch grid and four-and-a-half-inch walls in inches. They are a starting point for the room you are about to draw, not a standard — move a wall and the room follows.

    Choose Wall under Add to room, select a wall type, and choose Draw on the Plan. Drag from the start to the end; nearby wall endpoints snap together, otherwise the grid is used. Choose Cancel to leave drawing mode. Select a wall to edit its finished length or angle: its start stays fixed and joined walls follow. A warning names any joint that must remain open.

    A closed wall loop contains the cabinets. An open run has no enclosing boundary. Corner cabinets attach to square wall junctions inside the room, or travel along a wall. Use the Corner picker to choose a junction or Along a wall to release it.

    Cabinet properties for one room

    Each room follows the job's properties until you change them for that room. In Plan, open Items & room and choose Cabinet properties under Room settings: its row says Follows the job, or how many values the room changes. The sheet has the same pages as Job Properties: Standards, Fronts, Materials, Finish & Banding, Hardware, Joints and Assembly style.

    Change a value and it becomes the room's: a dot marks it, and Reset to job under it follows the job again. A dot beside a page says the room changes something on it, and Reset tab to job at the bottom puts the whole page back. A value the room has not changed keeps following the job, so a later change in Job Properties still reaches it. Units, cutting and parameters stay with the job.

    The nearest level wins: a cabinet's own settings, then the room's, then the job's. A cabinet placed in two rooms is built in each with that room's values, and when the rooms differ it is listed once for each room in the cut list, the bill of materials and the estimate.

    Standards holds the room's cabinet sizes, where its wall cabinets hang, and its legs and plinth. A new wall cabinet height re-hangs every wall cabinet in the room still following it; one whose height you typed stays where it is. Fronts holds the door and drawer-front styles, the small drawer-front cutoff, inner drawers and case tops.

    Assembly style gives the room a style of its own, such as framed cabinets in the kitchen and frameless in the laundry. Cabinets that follow the job's style are built with the room's wherever that room is drawn or listed, and joined face frames in the room follow it. A cabinet with a style of its own, or none, keeps it, and Design shows a cabinet as it is stored, with the job's style.

    Materials lists the sheets and boards in the job and, under Add from the shop, those in your shop library that the job lacks, such as poplar in a job made before it shipped: choosing one adds it to the job. The Materials rows of a cabinet in Design, and of its item settings, offer Add from the shop too.

    Sloped ceilings

    A sloped ceiling is set on the wall it slopes over. In Elevations, look at the wall and use Ceiling in its inspector; in Plan, select the wall and use its Sloped ceiling section. Pick a picture: Flat, Rises to the right, Rises to the left, Peak, or Slopes away from this wall. Heights are measured on the wall, and positions from its left end as you face it.

    Rises to the right and Rises to the left slope the wall's own top along its length, as on the end wall of an attic or a single wall under a sloping roof. Give the Low height, the High height, and where the slope starts, measured from the low end; the ceiling is level before it. Peak, often called a cathedral or vaulted ceiling, is two slopes meeting at a ridge: give the Left height, the Peak height, the Peak from the left and the Right height. The ceiling slopes the same way across the whole room.

    Slopes away from this wall is a knee wall: the ceiling rises from the wall's finished face, away from it, until it reaches the room height. Give the Height at the wall and the pitch as rise over run, such as 8 / 12, or type the Angle. The knee wall's elevation says how far out the ceiling reaches full height. A wall drawn inside the room has two faces; pick one by the way it faces, such as Faces north.

    On the elevation, drag the orange handles on the wall's top: the ends move up and down, and a slope's start or a peak's ridge also moves along the wall, catching on the room height and the middle of the wall. Each drag is one step to undo. Tap a handle to type its height. Where the wall's top is below the room height, the room height is drawn dashed.

    Slopes on two walls make an attic room: they meet in a ridge, or in a flat strip where both reach full height. The plan shades each slope from its knee wall to a dashed line where it reaches full height. Elevations draw each wall up to the ceiling, and in 3D the walls stop at it.

    A cabinet, countertop, door or window that runs into a slope is listed with the coordination issues. A wall cabinet hung below the ceiling keeps its gap at its lowest point under the slope. Crown molding, and a riser under it, stops where the slope starts.

    With Casework Pro, Fit to ceiling beside the issue, or under Edit item in the cabinet's inspector in Plan or Elevations, shapes the cabinet so its top follows the slope: the room's below-the-ceiling gap under it, or tight to it when wall cabinets hang from the floor. Placing, moving or resizing a cabinet into the slope asks whether to fit it. If the slope or the cabinet moves later, the issue comes back with Re-fit, which starts again from the cabinet as it was before it was fitted; changes made to it since are not kept.

    Fit builds the carcass to the slope. The Top lies on the slope with its ends cut plumb. A top that is flat and then slopes gets two Tops, or two sets of stretchers on a base, mitred at the break. Nailers drop under the slope. Adjustable shelves the slope crosses move down under it, and one that ends up closer than 6 in (150 mm) to the shelf below is taken out. Fixed shelves stay where they are and are cut to the slope. The Back stops under the Top and keeps its grooves. A drawer the slope crosses becomes a false front; the drawers below it stay.

    Fit refuses a cabinet at an angle to the knee wall, one with too little left under the slope, and one whose parts have no room, such as a tall whose nailers will not fit under the knee wall; shape it in the Box editor instead. It refuses corner cabinets too: build a corner under a slope as a custom cabinet. Casework says which it is.

    Fit to ceiling cuts a framed cabinet's face frame to the slope with the carcass, as Cabinets under a sloped ceiling or stair describes under Design a cabinet. A framed cabinet whose frame cannot follow is refused rather than built without its frame.

    Face frames in a run

    Casework checks framed cabinets against the walls and each other, and changes nothing until you tap. A framed cabinet whose side is within ½ in of a wall, and square to it, is listed with the coordination issues with Scribe stile, which widens the stile on that side by the style's Scribe allowance, on the wall side only. The same buttons are in the Face frame section of the cabinet's inspector.

    Two cabinets facing the same way with their fronts in line, one or both framed, with up to 3 in between their frames, get Close gap. As the style says, it widens both facing stiles by half the gap each, widens the one stile you choose by all of it, or adds a filler. A wider gap is only reported, unless the style closes gaps with a filler: use a filler or a wider cabinet. The cabinets never move, and their openings and fronts stay where they are; only the stiles' outside edges move.

    A filler belongs to the room, not to either cabinet. It is as wide as the gap and as tall as the shorter frame, cut from the frame's wood, drawn in the plan, the elevations and 3D, and listed under Fillers in the cut list and the estimate.

    When a cabinet or a wall moves, the issue comes back with Re-close or Re-scribe, and Put back takes the widening off or the filler out. A cabinet placed more than once in the job is copied for the place that changes, so the others stay as they were. A stile whose width you typed is never widened.

    A blind corner, framed or frameless, whose blind end meets a cabinet on the return wall is offered a pull-out filler, 3 in to start, so its door clears the return's doors and handles. Add a pull-out filler widens the blind panel to reach past the return and the filler; the door narrows and the cabinet keeps its width. If the return cabinet moves, Re-fit widens the panel again, and Put back restores it.

    Instead of closing the gap, two framed cabinets up to 3 in apart can share their frame at the seam. Joined face frames, next, explains how.

    Joined face frames

    Framed cabinets that stand side by side can share their frame at the seam, as a shop builds a run: one stile where two would butt. Each seam is Joined or Separate. The join belongs to the room, not to the cabinet, so the item itself does not change: placed somewhere else it keeps its own frame, and its item drawings show it on its own. A seam can be joined when both cabinets are framed, their fronts are in line, their top and bottom rails line up within 1 mm (about 1/32 in), and their frames are no more than 3 in apart.

    How a seam is joined is set in the assembly style, under Joining cabinets at the end of its Face frame page. One frame per run puts one frame across the joined cabinets: the top and bottom rails run the length of the run, and each seam gets one Joint stile, 1½ in to start, centred on it and wider by any gap. A rail longer than Longest rail, 96 in to start, is broken at the joint stile nearest the middle. Shared stile lets one cabinet's stile, as wide as the Joint stile, cover the seam, and the other cabinet's rails butt it. Split stile gives each cabinet a narrow stile, ¾ in to start, and the two read as one. Neighbours says whether cabinets that come to stand side by side are joined as you place them or kept separate; where two styles meet, the left cabinet's decides. At the starting sizes joining widens each opening beside the seam by ¾ in, so its doors and drawer fronts are cut wider.

    Elevations is the main place to join and split. A chain badge sits on each seam that can be joined, at the frame's middle height: filled when joined, dashed when separate. Tap it to join or split; the frame is redrawn at once, and each tap is one step to undo. Under Shared stile the first join asks which cabinet keeps its stile: Keep left or Keep right. Touch and hold the badge for Split, Width… to type this seam's joint stile, Keep left and Keep right, and on a run Break rails here, Don't break here and Automatic. Where a run's rails break, a tick crosses them; drag its scissors to another joint stile of the same run. The plan has the same badge on the front line at each seam.

    Select a framed cabinet and the Face frame section of its inspector has a Left and a Right row for each side that meets a framed neighbour, with the neighbour's name: choose Joined or Separate. A seam that can't be joined has no badge, and its row says why, such as rails that don't line up or a gap wider than 3 in. A joined seam that no longer fits, after a cabinet moves, keeps its badge with an exclamation mark: tap it to see why, and Split.

    Choose Edit item on a joined cabinet in Plan or Elevations, and in Design on Face it is drawn as it stands in the room: its share of each joint stile, the wider openings, and beyond each joined seam a faded stub of the neighbour's frame with its name. Tap the joint stile: it says Shared with the neighbour, and its Width is for this seam only, with Reset to style and Split beside it. On a run, tap the top or bottom rail to type its width; it is the run's, stored once for the whole run, and each cabinet's own width comes back if the run is split. Opened from Order Entry or the Item Library, the cabinet shows its own frame.

    Under One frame per run each run is a part of the job, a Frame run listed after the cabinets in the cut list, the bill of materials, the estimate and Face frame stock; its cabinets list no frame members of their own. Under Drawings it has one frame sheet with its own code, FR-1, FR-2 and so on: every member carries a number keyed to the table, a centre line marks where the rails break, and the note under the table says how the frame goes on, as the style's Run frame says: Attached in the shop, the boxes fastened together and the frame put on as one, or Applied on site, the frame shipped loose. No connector screws are listed. Under Shared stile and Split stile each cabinet keeps its own frame and is listed with how it is joined, such as joined right.

    A blind corner joins a run on its door end only; its blind end keeps its own stile, and a pull-out filler there stays a part of its own. Where two walls' runs meet at a blind corner they are two runs, with two frames and two sheets. Other corner cabinets, and frames cut to a sloped ceiling, stay separate.

    Jobs saved before cabinets could be joined keep every seam separate, and nothing in them moves. Where the style joins neighbours, the coordination issues offer Join this run for each run that could be joined. A seam you split stays split. Joining a seam whose gap was closed takes the widening or the filler off, and the joint stile bridges the gap; split it, and Close gap is offered again.

    Place and repeat objects

    Select a cabinet in Plan or Elevations and choose Edit item to customize it in the item editor. Choose Back to job to save and return to the same room and selection, then continue designing. The reusable library item is unchanged. If the job item represents several units, the edits apply to all of them.

    Use the Corners tag to find corner cabinets. The 90° and 45° corners come Hinged Left and Hinged Right; on a 90° corner the pull is on the door that folds, at its outer end. The frameless library includes an Over-range Upper — Door Pair, 30 × 18 × 12 in, with one adjustable shelf.

    Blind corners and lazy Susans are on the Corners tag too, and blind corners also carry the Blind corners tag: Joined face frames reads it to keep a blind end separate, so give a blind corner of your own that tag to have it treated as one. Base Blind Corner 42 and Upper Blind Corner 36 come Blind Left and Blind Right: a fixed blank panel closes the 24 in blind end, and the door is hinged on the blind side.     In Materials, Door styles, duplicate a style to create a shop standard and edit its rail and stile widths, panel and glass materials, joinery allowances, and prices. Customize this style edits the copy held by the selection you are looking at, without touching the library.

    The starter rail and stile widths, recesses, tongue and groove depth, and clearances are shop settings, not measurements taken from your construction. Set them to the way your shop actually builds a frame before relying on the cut list.

    Shape the profile

    Door profile sets what the cutter leaves behind. Inside contour shapes the step down to the panel, outside edge shapes the door’s own perimeter, and panel surface adds vertical grooves, reeding, or beadboard. The raised panel, the cathedral arch rise, an applied rim, a perimeter groove, and the columns and rows of a mullion front are all set here.

    Every profile dimension is a shop starting value, not a measurement taken off a cutter. The MDF presets keep the rail and stile widths and the panel setbacks their reference drawings state, and use editable starters for contour depths, corner radii, and joinery. Confirm the cutters, the glass templates, and the machining allowances before you cut. Check the pattern spacing and the groove positions on narrow fronts.

    What production receives

    A frame-and-panel front produces two stiles, two rails cut with the joinery allowance, and a centre panel. A routed frame and panel produces one frame blank with the opening cut through it plus a separate panel. A routed MDF blank produces one blank with a pocket. Glass styles produce a real opening plus a glass line under Doors and glass in the bill of materials. Purchased fronts are ordered rather than cut, and are left out of nesting and cutting DXF files.

    An applied rim is cut as its own blank, and the door under it loses the rim’s projection from its own thickness. A mullion front adds its vertical and horizontal bars. Profiled panels, arched rails, and mouldings arrive as cutting blanks: the shaping, the joinery, and the glass retention stay shop setup, and glass for a curved opening is a bounding blank rather than a finished template.

    Frame DXF files describe the cutting blanks. Joinery and glass retention remain shop setup. A front too small for its frame or insert is reported as an error and is left out of the cut quantities rather than being cut to an impossible size.

    A square-foot style price replaces the raw material and edge-banding cost for that front, and the per-front minimum applies to each door before quantity. Purchased fronts with no price, and glass with no price, are reported as missing prices rather than treated as free. Choose a real panel material, or the panel is priced as a separate stock with no price of its own.

    An MDF preset that calls for 22 mm or 25 mm board stays unpriced until your shop stocks that thickness or gives the style a door price. It never borrows the 19 mm sheet’s rate. Panels, thinner frame stock, and applied rims are priced as their own stock for the same reason.

    Joints and fastener spacing

    Set how many dowels or screws a joint takes, and where they sit.

    How many

    Dowelled, screwed, and pocket-screwed joints decide their fasteners in one of two ways. A set number uses the number you type, whatever size the part becomes. As many as the spacing needs adds one more fastener whenever two would be further apart than the Furthest apart distance, then spreads the row evenly.

    A spacing keeps working when a cabinet changes size; a count does not. With the shipped settings of 25 mm from each end and 250 mm furthest apart, a 600 mm carcass joint takes four dowels and a 1200 mm one takes six, with nothing retyped. Only the field the rule uses is shown.

    Where the row starts and ends

    In from the start and In from the end are separate distances, so a joint held 12 mm off the edge that shows and 50 mm off the end that does not is one setting. The two ends are measured along the machined part's own edge, not from the front of the cabinet.

    Set this on the joint's row on a part, or once on the preset in Joints. Changing a preset does not change joints already set to their own numbers.

    Numbers that do not work

    Casework reports these rather than correcting them, so the drawing always matches what you asked for. Setbacks that meet or cross on a short joint bore one fastener in the middle and raise a warning naming the joint. A furthest-apart spacing of zero or less falls back to the shop default and says so. A spacing that asks for more than 64 fasteners, or a count above 200, is capped and reports what it was asked for.

    Warnings appear in the Issues list with the joint's name. They do not block export, so review them before machining.

    Where the two parts meet

    Both parts of a joint are bored in the same places. How many fasteners there are, and where they sit, are worked out once from the length the two parts actually overlap — so a dowel has a hole facing it, including where the parts are different lengths, are offset along the joint, or are fitted the other way round.

    This means a joint where the parts barely overlap takes few fasteners, because that is the joint you have drawn. If a carcass looks under-fastened, check how far the two parts actually meet before changing the spacing.

    Two cases fall back to laying each part out on its own edge. A joint whose parts do not reach each other at all is reported in the Issues list. A part turned to an angle other than a quarter turn is positioned by the box around it rather than by its own edges, so its overlap cannot be measured and no warning is raised. Check the hole positions on the drawing for both.

    Set up your shop

    Configure materials, joints, hardware, rates, and document identity.

    The first launch

    The first time Casework opens it asks three things it cannot guess: the units you read in, the currency your estimates are written in, and whether the app is light or dark. Each one shows you what it produces as you tap it, and all three are in Settings afterwards — units and cabinet sizes under New job defaults, currency under Estimating, appearance under Preferences.

    Choosing millimetres also offers to start you from metric cabinet standards. It is an offer, not a consequence: a unit is how a size is written, and a standard is what the cabinet actually is. Reading in millimetres leaves a 34½ inch base at 34½ inches, written as 876.3 mm.

    Shop libraries

    Use Materials, Joints, and Hardware to match what your shop actually stocks and builds with. Each opens an index of categories, and each category has its own page and its own search; paint is a Materials category. New items and jobs copy these definitions so existing work does not change when the library changes later.

    Joints sets how a carcass is held together, including how many dowels or screws a joint takes and where they sit. See Joints and fastener spacing.

    Materials also holds Door styles, which set what doors and drawer fronts are made of and how they are priced. See Door and drawer-front styles.

    Restore the shipped list puts back only what that screen holds, so restoring materials leaves your joints and hardware as they are. It asks before it replaces anything and cannot be undone. Items and jobs already made keep their own copies either way.

    Set your costs

    An estimate is built from numbers you set. Settings, then Estimating, holds an hourly rate for manufacturing, finishing, assembly and installation, your markup on cost, the tax name and rate, and the terms printed under the total on a customer estimate.

    Each item stores the hours it takes in every department, and the job's quantities multiply them. For a shipped item, open its card's menu in Items, choose View details, then Edit estimating: your hours replace the system allowance, survive app updates, and apply to items you add or copy afterwards. Jobs and My Items already made keep the hours they have. For your own items, the same hours are under Edit details.

    Material costs come from your libraries: a sheet's price per sheet, an edge tape's price per linear metre and its waste, a paint's cost per area, a door style's price and the price of hardware. Change them under Materials and Hardware.

    Add your own materials

    To add a sheet, open Materials, choose Sheet goods, then Add a material and Sheet. Name it, enter its real thickness and how it is sold, such as 18mm on the rack, say whether it has a grain and can be painted, then set its price and the unit it is priced in. A job copies the library when it is made, so a new sheet is there for the next job.

    Edge banding works the same way: choose Add a tape, then give it a name, a thickness, a look, a price per linear metre and a waste percentage. The thickness is subtracted from the part to give the cut size on every edge the tape is assigned to.

    Paint has a name, a code, a colour, a sheen of matte, satin or gloss, and a cost per area. That cost is the whole finishing schedule, sealer and topcoats, in material only; finishing labour stays on the item's hours. Leave it empty to name the finish on a quote without pricing it.

    Shipped materials are locked. To change one, use the copy button beside it to make a copy of your own.

    Solid wood

    Solid wood is a material bought by the board foot rather than the sheet. Seven species ship with Casework: hard maple, white oak, walnut, red oak, cherry, poplar and alder, each three quarters of an inch finished, sold as 4/4 and priced per board foot. In Materials, Sheet goods, they are listed under Solid wood. To add your own, choose Add a material, then Solid wood.

    A board's thickness is the finished thickness its parts are milled to; 4/4 is only the label it is bought under. The waste allowance, 35% on the shipped species, covers milling, defects and offcuts, and it is the only gross-up: board feet are counted on each part's finished size.

    Assign solid wood like any other material: to drawer sides, backs and sub-fronts, doors, drawer fronts and finished ends, or to a single part in the inspector. The menus list the sheets first and a Solid wood section after them. A plinth is always cut from a sheet, so its menus offer sheets only.

    The cut list, the bill of materials and the CSV list solid wood in board feet: the net figure the parts add up to, and the figure to buy with the waste added. The estimate prices the figure to buy. Solid wood is never laid out on a sheet, so the cut diagrams leave it out and say how many solid-wood parts are on the cut list instead.

    A frame-and-panel door can take its stiles and rails from solid wood, and its centre panel from a sheet or from solid wood. In Materials, Door styles, open the style and choose a Frame material and a Panel material. Same as door uses the door's own material, as before; a style that carries its own panel shows that panel's name instead.

    Each species also ships at 5/4, milled to one inch, which is what a one-inch shelf nosing is ripped from. A nosing of another height is cut from its species milled to that height and priced per board foot like its board.

    A finished end can be made the way the doors are. Choose Door-style panel for the end, in the cabinet's Cabinet tab or on the assembly style's Finished ends page, and it is built in the door style. With 2¼″ Shaker — solid maple frame, which ships with Casework, the stiles and rails are hard maple, listed in board feet, and the panel is 3 mm ply in a groove, laid out on a sheet. The stile and rail widths, the panel thickness and the groove depth are the style's, in Materials, Door styles.

    Casework does not glue boards up: a part wider than a board is listed at its full width, and joining boards is left to the shop.

    Paint, stain and clear coat

    Materials, Finishes holds three kinds of finish: paints, stains and clear coats. Beside the paints, six ship with Casework: Natural, a clear coat, and the stains Golden Oak, Early American, Provincial, Espresso and Grey Wash. To add your own, choose Add a finish, then Paint, Stain or Clear coat; the Kind control in the editor can still change it.

    A stain has a tone and a strength. The tone is the colour the wood moves toward and the strength how far it goes: at a low strength the wood barely changes, at a high one the tone dominates and the grain still shows. The editor shows it on maple, oak, cherry and walnut. A clear coat shows the wood as it is, under the sheen you choose.

    Like paint, a stain or clear coat is one entry with one price per area for the whole schedule, stain and topcoats together, in material only. Its sheen is the topcoat's.

    Each material says what it takes: Can be painted offers paint, and Can be stained offers stain and clear coat. The shipped solid woods and plywoods can be stained and melamine and MDF cannot; hard maple, poplar and the plywoods can also be painted. A part is offered only the kinds its material takes. A finish already on a part stays when its material changes, and the inspector says so.

    The bill of materials and the estimate name a stain or clear coat by its kind, such as Stain · Golden Oak · CW-S02 · Satin, so it is never read as a paint of the same name, and item drawings name it in the title block. A job or item made before the stains shipped can still use them: its finish menus have an Add from the shop section.

    Export from the work

    Open Export from an item or job and choose the output you need. Casework prepares the file, then opens the system share sheet. Choose Files, AirDrop, Mail, a USB destination, or another app yourself.

    Production outputs — cut lists, drawings, nesting diagrams, and DXF — need Casework Pro; a row marked Pro opens the purchase screen instead. The project file and the job file are always free.

    Transfer one job

    In Jobs, open a job's actions menu and choose Export Job File. The .caseworkjob file includes its order items, job properties, room plans, and attached drawings. Download or reattach any missing drawing before exporting.

    On the receiving device, choose Import Job in Jobs and select the file. It opens as a separate job with a unique name; existing jobs and shop defaults stay as they are. Job files must be 256 MiB or smaller, with each drawing no larger than 25 MiB.

    Transfer one item

    One assembly travels as a .casework item file. Open Export from an item and choose the project file, which is always free, then share it as you would any other output.

    On the receiving device, choose Import Items in the Item Library and select one or more .casework files. Each arrives as a new item with its own identity, so importing the same file twice adds a second copy and nothing already on the device is replaced. If one file cannot be read, the whole selection is refused and nothing is added; the message names that file so you can import the rest without it. An item file must be 64 MiB or smaller, and one selection is limited to 100 files and to 64 MiB in total, so several large items can be refused together even though each would import on its own.

    DXF identity and names

    Job DXF filenames identify the order line, rooms, and cabinet; per-part files also name the part. The drawing carries this identity above the geometry on the IDENTITY layer by default. In Settings, DXF layers, use Order and room label to change its layer or turn the label off. Turning it off leaves the filename identity. Keep identity and part text layers out of machining operations. Export filenames and DXF text use ASCII approximations of names; long filenames are shortened and duplicates receive numbers. Saved names keep their original spelling.

    Cut diagrams and the cutting machine

    Cut diagrams follow the job's cutting machine, set in Job Properties under Cutting Machine. New jobs start from Settings, New job defaults, and an item opened on its own keeps its own. A beam saw lays each sheet out as edge-to-edge cuts, rip or crosscut first, in two to four stages, and numbers every cut in the order it is made. The exported cut diagrams give the size to set the saw to: it is written beside each cut where the strip is wide enough, and a cut sequence page after each sheet lists every cut with its size and length, so narrow strips and parts close together are never left unlabelled.

    A beam-saw diagram trims only the sheet edges that parts are packed against. An edge no part reaches is left alone, which saves a cut and leaves a larger offcut.

    When the saw cuts books, one diagram stands for a stack of identical sheets and says how many in red across the top: cut them together. A CNC router nests parts by their true outline, a cutter's diameter apart, and can place small parts inside a straight-sided cutout. The gap between parts applies to the saw only.

    Check before manufacture

    Review dimensions, material thicknesses, cutting gap, tool operations, grain direction, edge banding, and the DXF layer preview before sending files to a machine. A successful export is not a substitute for a shop drawing review or a machine dry run.

    Back up and restore

    Keep a portable copy of projects, jobs, libraries, and defaults.

    Create a backup

    Settings > Backup & iCloud > Create a backup creates one Casework backup containing every readable item and job on this device, plus your shop libraries, item tags, estimating settings, and new-job defaults. An unreadable item or job is left in place and skipped, so review the backup counts before relying on the file. Save that file somewhere separate from the device.

    Sync with iCloud

    Settings > Backup & iCloud can opt this device into private iCloud sync. Casework keeps a local working copy and synchronizes projects, jobs, attachments, and shop settings through your Apple account.

    Sync needs Casework Pro, and stops when Pro ends. Your local work stays on the device and this setting is remembered, so sync resumes where it left off if you subscribe again. Creating and restoring a backup is always free.

    If the same project or job changes independently on two devices, Casework keeps the iCloud version under the original name and preserves the other version as a dated Conflict copy. Review both and delete the one you no longer need.

    Restore safely

    Restore reads the file first and previews the result. Merge adds work whose identifier is not already on the device and leaves existing work and shop settings alone. Replace makes the device match the backup and can remove work that is not in it. Review the preview carefully before choosing Replace.

    Troubleshooting

    Recover from export, storage, and model problems.

    A change is refused

    Look for the conflict banner and select the relationships it names. Change or remove the conflicting relationship before trying the edit again. Casework does not force a part through constraints that define the construction.

    A file will not export

    Resolve any validation issues shown in the item, confirm that the model has parts, and check that the device has free storage. For DXF, review the layer templates in Settings > DXF layers and remove characters your downstream CAM software does not accept.

    If Casework says the output needs Pro, check Settings > Casework Pro. Choose Restore Purchases if you have subscribed on another device.

    Before asking for help

    Include the Casework version and build shown in About, the device and OS version, what you expected, what happened, and the smallest backup or exported item that reproduces the problem. Remove customer information you do not want to share.
